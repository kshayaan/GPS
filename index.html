The Stopwatch in Your Pocket

An interactive explainer · about 2 minutes

How does your phone know where you are?

This page shows how GPS works. First, 5 quick cards explain the idea. Then you try it yourself: you play the phone and find yourself on a map using satellites.

You use it every day

Open Maps, and a blue dot appears.

Nobody told your phone where you are. So how does it know? Let's find out in 2 minutes.

It takes two things

Satellites in space, and the GPS chip in your phone.

The satellites only send. Your phone only listens: it never sends anything back, so the satellites don't even know you exist.

Step 1

The satellites keep shouting the time.

About 30 of them circle the Earth, 20,200 km up. Each one keeps sending: "I'm here, and the time is exactly now."

Step 2

How late it arrives tells you how far away it is.

Radio travels at the speed of light, about 300 metres every millionth of a second. Your phone's stopwatch times the delay and turns it into a distance.

Step 3

Three distances meet at one spot. That's you.

One distance means you're somewhere on a circle. Two circles cross at two spots. A third picks the right one. Now try it on a real map.

Step 1 of 7

Challenges

    You get it.

    Your phone finds you with a stopwatch: how late each satellite's signal arrives tells it how far away that satellite is. And because that stopwatch is cheap, GPS treats its error as one more unknown and solves for it too.

    See the other problems GPS has to beat ↓

    Why pinpointing you is hard

    You've already beaten problem 1: the cheap stopwatch. Your phone has to beat these too. Each experiment runs the same solver on a harder case.

    Problem 2 · Satellites in the wrong part of the sky

    Every distance gets a small random error of about 5 m, like real signals do. We solve for your position 300 times with fresh errors each time. Watch how far the blue dot wanders.

    Problem 3 · A signal that bounced off a building

    In a city, a signal can reach you by bouncing off a tall building. It arrives late, so that one distance is too long. Can the phone tell which satellite is lying?

    Satellites heard:

    Problem 4 · The real world is 3D

    Real GPS has one more unknown, your height, so it needs at least 4 satellites. Here is the same solver at real scale, with your phone on the Earth's surface and its stopwatch off by a whole thousandth of a second.

    What's computed, what's drawn, what's simplified

    Computed: every distance, travel time, circle, blue dot, clock error, error cloud and table value comes from our own solver (gps.js), run in your browser from what you press. Drawn by hand: the streets, river and park, and the pictures on the intro cards. They are decoration only.

    Simplified: the main map is flat (2D), so 3 satellites are enough there; the satellites are placed on the map's edges instead of in orbit; they don't move; real receivers also correct for the atmosphere and for each satellite's own small clock error, which we leave out. Section 3 uses real distances but a fixed, made-up set of satellite directions.

    1. The solver is Gauss–Newton least squares: guess a position and clock error, compare the distances that guess predicts with the ones measured, correct the guess, repeat.
    2. How much the satellites' layout magnifies errors is called dilution of precision (DOP). Receivers prefer satellites spread across the sky.
    3. Catching a bad signal by leaving each satellite out in turn is the idea behind receiver autonomous integrity monitoring (RAIM), used in aircraft GPS.
    4. The GPS constellation is run by the United States; Europe (Galileo), Russia (GLONASS) and China (BeiDou) run their own, and most phones listen to several.
